Iron gall ink was the everyday writing ink of Europe for almost a thousand years. Fresh from the bottle it is *pale and greyish*, it turns black on the page within a day, and over the centuries it ==fades to a warm brown==, the colour of old letters and manuscripts. #ink

> The palest ink is better than the best memory.
>
> — *Chinese proverb*

## Making the ink

1. Crush oak galls and soak them in rainwater for a few days.
2. Stir in iron sulphate, once called *green vitriol*, and watch the liquid turn black.
3. Thicken it with gum arabic, so it flows evenly from the nib.

> [!tip] Testing a batch
> Write a line on scrap paper and leave it by the window. Good ink darkens overnight, weak ink stays grey.

## Why it fades

The iron in the ink keeps reacting with air, light and the paper itself.[^1] Archivists watch for three things:

- **Light**, which bleaches the black back to brown
- **Humidity**, which speeds up every reaction
  - above 50% the ink can start to spread
- **Acid**, left over from a ~~careful~~ careless recipe

| Ink | When fresh | After a century |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron gall | Blue-black | Warm brown |
| Carbon | Black | Black |
| Sepia | Brown | Paler brown |

## Writing with it

Iron gall ink suits dip pens best, it corrodes the steel of [[Fountain pens|fountain pen]] nibs over time.
